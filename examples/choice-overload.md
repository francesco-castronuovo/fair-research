# Illustrative example: choice overload

This is an **example of the questions Fair Research encourages**, not a study conducted with Fair Research and not a claim that this skill improves research outcomes.

## Define the question

Instead of asking broadly whether "more choice is bad," ask: "Under what conditions does increasing the number of options affect the probability of making a purchase?" Keep purchase rate separate from satisfaction, regret, and decision time.

## Discover explanations

Candidate alternatives include: larger assortments can inhibit selection under some conditions; assortments may have no consistent overall effect; and outcomes may depend on assortment complexity, decision difficulty, preference uncertainty, and decision goal. These claims may be partially compatible rather than mutually exclusive.

## Check evidence and dependencies

- Iyengar and Lepper (2000), *When Choice Is Demotivating: Can One Desire Too Much of a Good Thing?* https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11138768/
- Scheibehenne, Greifeneder, and Todd (2010), *Can There Ever Be Too Many Options? A Meta-Analytic Review of Choice Overload*. https://doi.org/10.1086/651235
- Chernev, Böckenholt, and Goodman (2015), *Choice Overload: A Conceptual Review and Meta-Analysis*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002

Do not assume the two meta-analyses provide independent underlying observations. Inspect their included-study lists and trace shared data before assigning weight. The exact overlap has not been audited in this candidate.

The 2015 paper has a [corrigendum](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2015.07.001), published online in 2015 and in the April 2016 issue, correcting statistical expressions on p. 348. The candidate uses the publisher abstract for its moderator description, not uncorrected numerical expressions. The retained preparation ledger is not bundled with this public package.

## Evaluate fairly

Check what each study actually measured, where its results apply, whether effects replicate independently, and which outcomes cannot be compared directly. Investigate whether alternate defensible scope decisions would change the interpretation.

## Report cautiously

The point of the example is not to declare one universally correct rule about assortment size. It is to demonstrate why defining the outcome, discovering contextual explanations, and checking evidence overlap can improve the *inspectability* of a conclusion. The improvement itself has not been empirically established for this skill.
